Is Osaka even the right call?

Fair question — you asked from scratch, so it's worth answering honestly instead of assuming. I weighed it against the two other realistic winter picks from Korea: Fukuoka (the shortest hop) and Hokkaido (the only one with actual snow).

Note: the table below is the original Osaka-only comparison from when this dossier started. The plan has since grown to include Tokyo + Mt Fuji (§07) — see §05 for the current, up-to-date budget.

💭 My honest take: stick with Osaka. Given "cheaper is better" has been the brief the whole way through, it's genuinely the best value of the three — cheapest flights, and Kyoto + Nara give you more range than Fukuoka's day trips for similar or less money. Fukuoka's only real edge is an even shorter flight — worth it only if you'd rather eat and soak than sightsee. Hokkaido is the one honest upgrade: it's real winter, snow included, which Osaka's 5–12°C simply isn't. But budget roughly doubles, and the Snow Festival itself runs Feb 4–11, 2027 — right on top of Seollal, so you'd be stacking two price surges at once. If a "real winter" trip is the actual goal, that's worth planning as its own trip later with a bigger budget, not squeezed into this one.

Compare across all of these at once on Naver Flight, Skyscanner or Trip.com rather than one airline's own site — LCC seat sales don't always show up until you filter by date.

Also worth checking — flying out of Gimpo (GMP) instead

Gimpo does run a Kansai route: Korean Air, Asiana and Jeju Air each fly it twice daily, plus Peach four times daily — about 14 departures a day combined, same ~1h45m flight. Off-peak, with an airline coupon, fares can dip into the high ₩100,000s per person — in the same ballpark as the Incheon LCCs above.

The real draw isn't the ticket price, it's the airport itself: Gimpo sits inside the city, ~20–30 min from most of Seoul, versus 60–90 min out to Incheon. For a group of 6 that's a real chunk of time and transfer cost saved, especially on an early flight straight after your last paper.

The trade-off — T'way, the cheapest carrier in the table above, doesn't fly this route, and departure times cluster mid-morning to evening rather than spreading out, so there's less room to chase an ultra-cheap red-eye.

🧭 My take: if any of you actually live near Gimpo or central Seoul, check Jeju Air / Peach on GMP–KIX before booking — the time and transfer money saved can outweigh a slightly higher fare. Purely on ticket price alone, though, Incheon still has the edge, since it has more carriers fighting for your seat.

Kereta sewa — do you actually need one?

Short answer: no, not for this itinerary — but here's the honest case for and against, plus what you'd need if you go for it anyway.

Can a Malaysian license even drive in Japan?

Yes. Japan accepts International Driving Permits issued under the 1949 Geneva Convention, and Malaysia's JPJ issues exactly that format. Apply at any JPJ branch with your Malaysian license, passport copy and a photo — RM150, usually same-day, valid 1 year. You carry both the IDP and your original Malaysian license together; the IDP alone isn't valid.

What it would cost
Compact car, per day¥6,000–9,000 (≈₩55,000–80,000)
Osaka ⇄ Kyoto expressway toll, round trip¥7,000–9,000
City-centre parking (Kiyomizu, Gion, Dotonbori)¥500–1,000 / hour
Fuel, 4-day trip≈¥5,000
⚠️ Why I'd skip it for this trip: every stop on the route below — Dotonbori, Osaka Castle, Fushimi Inari, Gion, Nara's deer park — sits right on a train line, and Kyoto's old streets are narrow, one-way, and short on parking. For 6 people, a shared IC card and the odd taxi will beat a rental car on cost and hassle once you add tolls and parking on top of the daily rate — and remember, a "6-seater" that actually fits 6 adults plus a week of luggage is rarer than it sounds.

Where a car earns its keep: a detour the train doesn't reach well, like Arima Onsen or the Awaji Island coast for a half-day. If that's tempting, it'd slot in as a swap for the Nara/USJ day rather than a whole extra rental for the trip — worth a follow-up plan if you want to chase that.

✅ Same call for the Mt Fuji leg (§07): a one-way car rental Osaka↔Tokyo/Fuji area got priced out too — one-way drop-off fees for a route this long run ¥50,000–90,000+ on top of toll and fuel, which lands at or above 6 shinkansen tickets while taking twice as long and adding real winter-driving fatigue. The final plan uses overnight buses instead, which turned out cheaper than even the train. Details in the budget below.

The route — Osaka ⇄ Tokyo ⇄ Mt Fuji, 6 nights 7 days

One loop, no backtracking: Osaka first, an overnight bus up to Tokyo, two days there, a day trip out to the Kawaguchiko Fuji photo spot, then a direct overnight bus straight back to Osaka — skipping the Mishima/Nagoya shinkansen transfer entirely. A full Kyoto/Nara/USJ day didn't fit without cutting Tokyo or Fuji short, so Kyoto gets a half-day on Day 6 for Kiyomizu-dera. Full airport-to-airport schedule with exact times, every meal and every solat slot is in the companion Excel file — this is the condensed version.

⚠️ Day 5, on the bus, is the real challenge. Both overnight buses (Osaka→Tokyo, Kawaguchiko→Osaka) cross a Subuh window while still moving. Bring water/tayammum-safe wipes, a qiblat app, and don't be afraid to pray seated or to jamak/qada once you land if it genuinely isn't possible on board.
Where the halal food actually is

Osaka: Gyomu Super has several halal-stocked branches (Nippombashi, Minamitsumori, Bentencho, Momodani) — meat, rice, spices, snacks; Halal Ramen Naniwaya near Namba does bento from ¥1,200 and a wagyu rice bowl around ¥3,000.

Tokyo: Tokyo Camii (Yoyogi-Uehara) is a full mosque with a halal market and Turkish food attached — worth routing a Zohor through. Shin-Okubo also has a cluster of halal-friendly Turkish/Middle Eastern spots.

Kawaguchiko: essentially nothing. It's a small mountain tourist town — bring bekal from Tokyo the night before, no exceptions.

🍳 On "masak je supaya jimat": honest answer — cooking only realistically works on the Osaka guesthouse nights (Day 2 and Day 6, kitchen confirmed). Days 3–5 you're in Tokyo manga cafes or on a bus, with zero kitchen access, so those meals are eat-out or bekal regardless of intent. Budget-wise that's roughly 40% cooked, 60% bought — still worth doing (₩16,200/person in groceries instead of eating out for those same meals), just don't expect it to cover the whole week.

Book these now — before seats/slots run out

Most of this trip is walk-up (temples, markets, Dotonbori). A handful of things aren't — they're either capacity-limited (a bus has a fixed number of seats) or timed-entry (a ticket for a specific hour). Miss the window on these and you're either stuck, or paying a lot more for a same-day scramble. Here's the honest priority order.

Priority 1 — book this week

The two overnight buses (Willer Express Osaka→Tokyo, Day 2; Fujiyama Liner Kawaguchiko→Osaka, Day 5)

These aren't optional add-ons — they're your transport and your accommodation for those nights. If either sells out, you're not just missing a fun extra, you're stuck rebuilding the whole route (extra hotel night, more expensive shinkansen, knock-on schedule changes for everyone).

Japan highway bus operators typically open bookings around 3 months before departure — for a Jan 6–12 trip, that window is opening right about now (late Sept/early Oct). Two things stack risk on top of that: Jan 6–12 sits right at the tail end of Japan's New Year travel rush (many locals are still moving around through the first week of Jan, and Japanese school winter break often runs into early/mid-Jan), and the Fujiyama Liner specifically is a smaller, single-daily direct service — fewer seats per departure than the mass Osaka↔Tokyo routes, so it's the one most likely to sell out first.

✅ Worth it? Not really a question — these ARE the plan, not an optional extra. Book via willer-travel.com (English site, card payment) as soon as your headcount and dates are locked. Book all 6 seats in one go if the site allows it, so you're guaranteed to sit together.
Priority 2 — book 1–2 weeks ahead

Shibuya Sky (Day 4, currently listed as optional)

It's a timed-entry ticket, not a walk-up queue — you book a specific hour through the official site. Current pricing: ¥2,700 (~₩24,300) online before 3pm, ¥3,400 (~₩30,600) from 3pm onward, +¥300 more if you buy at the counter instead of online (assuming there's anything left to buy). Sunset slots — the ones actually worth going for — sell out first, sometimes days ahead on clear-weather evenings.

💭 Worth it? Yes, if it survives the budget trim — 360° open-air Tokyo view with Fuji visible on a clear winter day is genuinely one of the better skyline views in the city, better than most indoor observation decks for the same price bracket. If you're doing it, book online for a sunset slot the moment you know your Day 4 plan, not the morning of. If it gets cut for budget, no regrets — it's correctly flagged as the first thing to drop in §05.
Priority 3 — optional, consider adding

teamLab Planets or Borderless (not currently in the itinerary)

Two separate venues now: teamLab Planets (Toyosu — barefoot, walk-through water rooms, structured route) and teamLab Borderless (Azabudai Hills — shoes-on, open wander, no fixed path). Dynamic pricing from about ¥3,600 (~₩32,400) per adult, rising on busy days. Planets tickets are never sold at the door — online only, always. Borderless sometimes has same-day counter tickets if inventory's left, at a small surcharge. January is off-peak compared to sakura/Golden Week/summer, so sellout risk is lower than the "book weeks ahead" warnings you'll see online, but weekend slots (your Day 4, a Saturday) still go first.

🧭 Worth it? If anyone in the group hasn't done a teamLab before, it's a genuinely different kind of attraction — immersive digital art, very photogenic, not "just another museum." The honest catch: it's not currently budgeted, and Day 4 only has room for one big timed-entry thing alongside everything else planned. If it replaces Shibuya Sky rather than stacking on top, it's a wash on both time and cost — worth a group vote on which one people actually want more.
Lower risk, still worth doing early

Manga cafe booths, Tokyo (Days 3–4)

Individual booths rarely "sell out" city-wide, but a specific branch can run short on private/group booths on a Friday or Saturday night in a busy area like Shinjuku or Shibuya — exactly your Day 3/4 nights. Most chains (Gran Cyber Cafe, Media Cafe Popeye, Manboo) let you reserve online or by phone.

💭 Worth it? Reserve the branch and booth type (flat-mat rooms fit a group better than single reclining seats) a few days ahead rather than the same day — low effort, removes a real "where do we sleep tonight" risk for 6 people arriving off an overnight bus.

Everything else in the itinerary — Osaka Castle, Kuromon Market, Dotonbori, Asakusa, Tokyo Camii, the Kawaguchiko Fuji viewpoints — is free or walk-up, no booking risk. The Osaka Amazing Pass (§05) is sold at machines/stations same-day and doesn't sell out.

Ways to trim it further

01

Fly Tue/Wed/Thu. Weekend departures from ICN run noticeably higher on every LCC — shifting either leg by a day can save ₩20,000–40,000 a head.

02

Skip the rail pass entirely. With Tokyo↔Osaka now running on overnight buses instead of shinkansen (§05/§07), a JR pass wouldn't pay for itself — a rechargeable ICOCA/Suica for city metro is all you need on top of the bus tickets.

03

Conbini breakfast on the days you must eat out. Halal-safe picks (salmon/tuna onigiri, bread) run ¥600–800 versus ¥1,500+ for a sit-down café — over the Tokyo/travel mornings for 6 people that's a real line item, not a rounding error.

04

Confirm the room before you pay, not after. A single "sleeps 6" listing is how you got this price — message the host for photos of the actual bedding setup so nobody's finding out about the floor situation at midnight on Jan 6.
